Tiny Recurrent Gardens: A Synthetic Paper for Offline Demos
Ada Example, Ben Placeholder

Abstract
This is a short, fictional paper written only to exercise the paper2pod pipeline without network
access. It describes Tiny Recurrent Gardens (TRG), an imaginary method that grows a small recurrent
network one cell at a time. On a toy benchmark of 1,000 synthetic sequences, TRG reaches 91.5%
accuracy while using 40% fewer parameters than a fixed-size baseline. None of these results are real.

1 Introduction
Recurrent networks are often sized by guesswork. Practitioners pick a hidden size, train, and hope
the choice was reasonable. If the network is too small it underfits; if it is too large it wastes
memory and overfits short datasets. In this paper we ask a simple question: can a network decide its
own size while it trains? We answer it with a deliberately small idea. TRG starts with four hidden
cells and adds a new cell whenever the validation loss stops improving for three consecutive epochs.
Cells that receive almost no gradient are pruned. The result is a model that grows where the data
needs capacity and stays small elsewhere.

2 Related Work
Growing networks have a long history, from constructive cascade methods to modern neural
architecture search (NAS). Pruning methods such as magnitude pruning remove weights after training.
TRG combines both directions inside one training loop, but it is far simpler than NAS: it never
trains more than one candidate model at a time.

3 Method
3.1 Growth Rule
Every epoch we record the validation loss. When it has not improved by at least 0.5% for three
epochs, TRG adds one hidden cell. The new cell is initialised with small random weights so that the
existing function of the network is barely disturbed.

3.2 Pruning Rule
After each epoch we measure the average gradient norm that reached each cell. Cells below 1% of the
median norm are removed. Pruning and growth are never applied in the same epoch, which keeps the
training curve stable.

4 Experiments
We evaluate on a toy benchmark of 1,000 synthetic sequences of length 50, split 800 for training and
200 for testing. The fixed-size baseline uses 64 hidden cells. TRG ends training with 38 cells on
average across 5 random seeds. It reaches 91.5% test accuracy against 90.8% for the baseline, using
40% fewer parameters. Training time is roughly the same because the growth checks are cheap.

5 Discussion and Limitations
The benchmark is tiny and synthetic, so these numbers say nothing about real tasks. The growth rule
depends on a patience value of three epochs, and a different patience could change the final size.
We also did not test TRG on long sequences, where recurrent models are known to struggle.

6 Conclusion
TRG shows that a recurrent network can choose its own size with two simple rules: grow when progress
stalls and prune what is unused. The idea is easy to implement and easy to explain, which is exactly
why it makes a good demo paper.
